Designer

Saul Bass

Motion / Identity American 1920 - 1996

Saul Bass changed what a film could do before it started. His title sequences for Hitchcock, Preminger, and Scorsese weren't opening credits, they were arguments, moving pieces of graphic design that put you inside a film's nervous system before a single actor appeared. He brought the Bauhaus sensibility he absorbed from Gyorgy Kepes at Brooklyn College into the movie industry, and the movie industry was never the same. Outside cinema he built some of the most enduring corporate identities in American commerce, AT&T, United Airlines, Quaker Oats, Girl Scouts, logos so confident and cleanly resolved they've barely been touched in half a century. He operated across two worlds that don't usually speak to each other and mastered both.

Signature Traits

  • Reduction to the irreducible. A figure. A line. A single arresting form that contains the whole emotional argument.
  • Motion as meaning. His title sequences move because the movement is the message, not decoration.
  • The poster as doorway. Every film campaign he designed made the film seem more itself than it had any right to be.
  • Corporate identity treated with the same rigor as fine art. A logo is a position statement, and his were bulletproof.
  • Collaboration as amplification. His work with Hitchcock and later Scorsese functioned as genuine creative dialogue, not service.

Major Works

  • Vertigo title sequence (1958)

    Spirals from a human eye. Bernard Herrmann's score underneath. The most psychologically precise opening in cinema history.

  • The Man with the Golden Arm (1955)

    A jagged arm. White bars. Jazz. It invented the modern film title sequence and nobody has fully surpassed it.

  • AT&T logo (1983)

    The Death Star globe, his critics called it. Fifty years later it is still the company's face. That is what correct looks like.

  • Saul Bass: A Life in Film & Design (Monograph)

    Written by his daughter Jennifer Bass with Pat Kirkham. The fullest account of his career ever assembled, spanning title sequences, corporate identity work, and the short films he made purely for himself.
